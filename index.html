Abstract

Product defects in broiler chicken processing reduce carcass quality, yield, and market value. PT XYZ measures performance but has not mapped the patterns and dominance of defect types. We combine Pareto and Ishikawa diagrams, the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP), and Fuzzy Failure Mode and Effect Analysis (Fuzzy FMEA) to analyze 31 defect types and 1,271,412 cases from April 2025 to March 2026. Five defect types account for 61.4% of all defects. The Ishikawa (6M) analysis traces the most frequent causes to human and machine factors. Five respondents produced a consistent aggregated AHP matrix (CR = 0.012), with AC Trachea (0.2455) and AM Heart Remnants (0.1106) carrying the highest weights. We rank 15 process lines with Fuzzy FMEA. Line 9A all machines (FRPN 618), Line 6A plucking (FRPN 562), and Line 9C all machines (FRPN 551) occupy the top three, the same as conventional RPN. Line 5A scalding rises from sixth to fourth and Line 8B evisceration from eighth to fifth, while Line 9B drops from fourth to eighth. We recommend machine maintenance and calibration, operator training and rotation, and upstream handling improvements.

Keywords: product defects, broiler chicken, Pareto diagram, AHP, Fuzzy FMEA.

1 | Introduction

Chicken is among the most widely consumed sources of animal protein in the world, and modern production systems are becoming increasingly efficient [1]. Production is expected to keep rising, with growth concentrated in developing countries [2]. Broiler profitability depends on feed prices, chick prices, and rearing technology [2]. Poultry slaughterhouses run a complex process. Quality varies because workers perform many stages manually, and because machines, work methods, and perishable raw material add further variation.

This complexity increases the risk of product defects, especially bruising and hemorrhage on the carcass surface. Electrical stunning before slaughter changes the amount and distribution of bleeding, pH levels, and meat color [3]. Defects such as woody breast and white streaks occur at different rates across poultry companies [4]. Initial observations at PT XYZ found 31 defect types, but no one has measured which types dominate or how much each contributes to the total. This study identifies the dominant defect types and causes in PT XYZ's broiler products using Pareto and Ishikawa diagrams, determines the priority weights of defect criteria with the Analytical Hierarchy Process (AHP), assesses and ranks failure risks and defect causes with Fuzzy FMEA, and formulates quality improvement strategies from the combined results of the three tools.

To date, PT XYZ has managed defects with Overall Equipment Effectiveness (OEE), a metric that assesses equipment productivity [5]. This approach treats all defect types as equally urgent and does not analyze operator errors, so defects are judged only from the machine side. A more effective FMEA needs to address effectiveness, difficulty, and practicality, and handle the information uncertainty that conventional RPN does not capture [6]. Pareto and Ishikawa have been used together to identify causes of poultry meat defects [7], and quality control tools have also been used to reduce defects in other manufacturing settings [8]. FTA and FMEA have been used to analyze product defects in the packaging process [9]. AHP derives criteria weights from experts' pairwise comparisons, while Fuzzy FMEA addresses the weaknesses of conventional RPN, namely that different combinations of S, O, and D can yield the same RPN and that the three factors are treated as equal [6].

Although each method has been widely applied, earlier studies generally use only part of this method chain and rarely rely on real defect data from the poultry processing industry. This study fills that gap. Its main contributions are:

  1. A five-stage framework linking statistical defect mapping, AHP weighting, Fuzzy FMEA risk assessment, and the formulation of mitigation measures.
  2. Quantitative mapping of 31 defect types (1,271,412 cases) and their causes using Pareto and Ishikawa 6M.
  3. Priority weighting of 11 dominant defect types based on the assessments of five respondents, with a consistency test.
  4. Evaluation of 15 process lines with Mamdani Fuzzy FMEA (125 rules) and a comparison with conventional RPN.
  5. Measurable mitigation recommendations for high-priority lines.

Section 2 reviews the literature and research gaps. Sections 3–4 (methodology and results) are presented as an interactive model below. Section 5 discusses implications and limitations. Section 6 concludes and outlines directions for further research.

2 | Literature Review

The relevant literature covers three areas: statistical quality control for prioritizing product defects, FMEA and its fuzzy extensions for assessing failure risk, and AHP for weighting evaluation criteria. Each domain has one weakness: root cause analysis stops at conjecture, risk assessment relies on subjective scores, and weighting does not account for uncertainty. We combine the three in one framework.

2.1 | Product Defects and Statistical Quality Control Tools

A defective product is one that does not meet the specification or quality standard set [9]. Statistical quality control (SQC) has two main components: acceptance sampling and statistical process control (SPC). Acceptance sampling determines whether a lot can be accepted without full inspection [15]. SPC uses control charts to monitor production in real time [16] and keeps the process stable by reducing variability. In the cosmetics industry, combining SPC and Pareto analysis reduced the defect rate by up to 50 percent according to one report [17].

To prioritize improvement, practitioners pair the Pareto diagram with Ishikawa [7], [8]. The Pareto diagram ranks defect categories by contribution and points to the most influential cause. The Ishikawa diagram groups potential causes into six 6M categories: man, machine, material, method, measurement, and environment. In one manufacturing case study, Pareto analysis showed size error as the dominant defect at 73.17 percent, and Ishikawa traced it to man, material, method, and machine [18]. In piston casting, a Pareto-Lorenz diagram isolated the top three defects, which made up about 80 percent of the problem. The team then drew a separate Ishikawa for each defect to find causes and propose corrective actions [20].

Together, the two tools give a targeted, evidence-based strategy: Pareto shows which problem matters most, and Ishikawa shows why it occurs [19]. Several studies add FMEA. The casting study used Ishikawa, Pareto-Lorenz, and FMEA to identify the main causes of nonconformity, their frequency, and their risk [21]. Causes listed in Ishikawa remain hypotheses, so teams must validate them with data and field observation [8]. On a mobile phone assembly line, Pareto analysis of one month of rework data showed that 24 of 99 defect codes accounted for 79.81 percent of rework. After the team addressed the validated causes, rework fell from 20,188 to 10,976 units [8].

2.2 | FMEA, Fuzzy FMEA, and AHP

FMEA is a structured procedure for identifying and preventing failure modes. The team subjectively rates severity (S), occurrence (O), and detection (D) on a 1–10 scale and multiplies them into a risk priority number, RPN = S × O × D [10]. RPN has three weaknesses: it lacks a strong mathematical basis, its distribution is not continuous, and different combinations of S, O, and D produce identical values [22]. Experts interpret the rating criteria differently, which adds inaccuracy [23]. RPN gives the three factors equal weight and ignores the uncertainty in expert judgments [24].

Fuzzy FMEA addresses these problems. It converts linguistic ratings into fuzzy sets, applies IF-THEN rules, and defuzzifies the result into a Fuzzy RPN (FRPN) [11], [12]. Cardiel-Ortega and Baeza-Serrato built a Mamdani fuzzy system with 27 rules and validated it at a textile company [23]. Subramanian et al. proposed an improved trapezoidal fuzzy model for risk prioritization [25]. Sabripoor et al. combined Fuzzy FMEA with Fuzzy Hybrid MCDM to rank 20 hazards after organ transplantation, and found immunosuppressant non-adherence to be the most critical [29].

The second strategy weights the risk factors. Efe used a fuzzy AHP extension to set priorities for O, S, and D, then ranked failure modes with fuzzy TOPSIS [26]. Jin et al. weighted factors with AHP in a fuzzy FMEA-AHP model for logistics systems [27]. Chang et al. built a fuzzy AHP-based RPN because traditional RPN does not prioritize risk factors [28].

Saaty's AHP derives weights from pairwise comparisons on a 1–9 scale and checks the result with the Consistency Ratio (CR) [13]. Bognár and Benedek combined AHP with risk assessment methods and tested the consistency of expert panel judgments [30]. AHP relies on precise numerical judgments, so it is weak at capturing the uncertainty of subjective evaluation [14]. Because pairwise judgments are subjective, researchers often pair AHP with fuzzy logic. A bibliometric study of 2,086 articles places fuzzy AHP among the most widely used methods [31]. Real-world information is uncertain, and one study compared the stability of several fuzzy AHP variants [32].

5 | Discussion

Defect frequency and defect importance do not align. The three feather defects (AP) occur most often (12.9%, 12.6%, and 12.5% of cases), but their AHP weights are only 5.09%, 5.86%, and 8.12%, ranking them 9th, 8th, and 6th. AC Trachea, ranked 4th in frequency (11.7%), receives the highest weight, 24.55%. The largest shifts occur for Broken Chicken (rank 10 to 3) and AM Heart Remnants (rank 8 to 2), while AM Crop Remnants falls from rank 5 to 10. Pareto alone directs attention to plucking, since three of the top five defects originate there. Expert judgment shifts part of the priority to internal organ defects (AC Trachea and AM Heart Remnants, which arise during evisceration) and to physical damage (Broken Chicken, Wing Bruise, Thigh Bruise). According to Ishikawa, this physical damage is linked to crate unloading, hanging, the plucker machine, and stunning voltage.

In the Ishikawa analysis, human and machine factors each contribute four of the 16 general causes. FMEA agrees: the top three lines are all machine-related, namely Line 9A (FRPN 618), 6A (562), and 9C (551). Upstream stages carry lower risk. Lines 1A–4A have FRPN 319–443 and rank 10th to 15th (3A 10th, 1A 12th, 4A 13th, 2A 15th). The OEE approach used by PT XYZ looks only at the machine side, so it does not capture operator-related causes such as lack of training (Line 8B, FRPN 530) and inexperience (Line 8A, FRPN 509). Current controls are mostly reactive or detection-based: repair and replacement after breakdown, unscheduled calibration, verbal warnings, start-of-shift briefings, and supervisor oversight. Lines 3A and 10A have no controls at all. These controls need to shift toward prevention.

FRPN ranks lines similarly to RPN (Spearman correlation 0.94). Nine of the 15 lines have the same rank under both methods, and the top three are identical (9A, 6A, 9C). The top-five overlap is three of five lines: Lines 5A and 8B enter the top five only under FRPN, while Lines 9B and 10A drop out. Line 5A has the highest severity (6.6) with moderate occurrence (3.8) and detection (4.2). Multiplication (RPN 105.34) places it 6th, whereas FRPN raises it to 4th. Line 8B (S = 6.2; O = 4.0; D = 3.8) rises from 8th to 5th, so it deserves more attention than RPN suggests. Line 9B falls from 4th to 8th: its O (5.0) and D (5.8) are high, but its S (4.6) is below the 15-line mean (5.4). This pattern shows that FRPN gives severity more weight than plain multiplication. FRPN also widens the gaps between lines: Lines 9A and 6A differ by 3.17 points in RPN and 56 points in FRPN. Studies of Fuzzy AHP-based RPN report a similar pattern, namely a wider range of values and rankings that differ from conventional FMEA [19], [21].

The model combines FRPN and AHP in an integrated score = α × (FRPN / maximum FRPN) + (1 − α) × (AHP score / maximum AHP score). At α = 0.60, the top five are Lines 8B, 9A, 8A, 9C, and 10A. Line 8B rises from FRPN rank 5 to first, Line 8A from 7th to 3rd, and Line 8C from 11th to 6th, because all three map to internal organ defects with high weights (AHP score 11.71%). Line 6A falls from 2nd to 10th and Line 5A from 4th to 12th, because both relate only to feather defects with low weights (6.36% and 5.85%). Lines 9A, 9B, 9C, 10A, and 10B are mapped to all defects, so their AHP score equals the mean (9.09%). Machines remain important (9A 2nd, 9C 4th), but expert weighting lifts evisceration to the front. The drop of 6A and 5A does not mean their risk is small: feather defects are the most frequent and both lines have high FRPN, so both still need improvement.

This study has four limitations. First, it covers one company and five internal respondents, so generalization is limited, and the S, O, D scores, as mean respondent ratings, remain subjective. Second, the mapping of lines to defect types is an initial assumption, and α = 0.60 was chosen without a sensitivity test, so the integrated ranking depends on both. Third, the researchers set the membership functions and rule base (125 rules) without sensitivity analysis and without validation against post-improvement data, so the superiority of FRPN over RPN has not been proven empirically. Fourth, the analysis does not cover cost, implementation, or post-implementation evaluation of the recommendations.

6 | Conclusion

This study fills the gap left by earlier research, which used only part of the method chain and rarely used real defect data from poultry processing. It combines Pareto and Ishikawa diagrams, AHP, and Fuzzy FMEA in a five-stage framework and applies it to broiler chicken defects at PT XYZ. The conclusions follow the four research objectives.

First, defect mapping shows that a handful of types dominate. PT XYZ recorded 31 defect types and 1,271,412 cases between April 2025 and March 2026. The top five types account for 61.4 percent of cases: AP Coarse Feathers (12.9%), AP Fine Feathers (12.6%), AP Epidermis (12.5%), AC Trachea (11.7%), and AM Crop Remnants (11.6%). The cumulative share reaches 82.6 percent at the 11th type, AM Liver Remnants, so we define 11 dominant defects. The share more than halves at the 6th type, Wing Bruise (4.5%), so improvement should target the top five. The Ishikawa analysis identified 16 general potential causes: four each for man and machine, three for material, two each for method and environment, and one for measurement. Improvement needs to start with operator competence and equipment condition.

Second, AHP weighting shows that defect frequency and importance differ. The aggregated matrix of five respondents is consistent (CR = 0.0124), and individual CRs range from 0.01 to 0.06, below the 0.1 threshold. AC Trachea has the highest weight (24.55%), followed by AM Heart Remnants (11.06%), Broken Chicken (10.45%), Wing Bruise (10.03%), and Thigh Bruise (9.25%). These five hold 65.3 percent of the total weight. The three feather (AP) defects are the most frequent but rank sixth (AP Epidermis), eighth (AP Fine Feathers), and ninth (AP Coarse Feathers) by weight. Pareto alone would draw attention to plucking, whereas expert judgment shifts part of the priority to internal organ defects and physical damage.

Third, Mamdani Fuzzy FMEA (125 rules) ranks the 15 process lines. Line 9A (FRPN 618), Line 6A (562), and Line 9C (551) occupy the top three, in the same order as conventional RPN. The Spearman correlation between RPN and FRPN is 0.94, and nine of the 15 lines have the same rank under both methods. Line 5A rises from sixth to fourth and Line 8B from eighth to fifth, while Line 9B falls from fourth to eighth and Line 10A from fifth to sixth. The FRPN top five is therefore Lines 9A, 6A, 9C, 5A, and 8B, and its overlap with the RPN top five is three of five lines. FRPN separates lines with close RPN values more sharply: Lines 9A and 6A differ by 3.17 points in RPN and 56 points in FRPN. Line 5A has the highest severity (6.6), and FRPN lifts it into high priority. Lines 1A–4A rank 10th to 15th (FRPN 319–443), so the upstream stages carry lower risk.

Fourth, combining FRPN and AHP at α = 0.60 yields five integrated priorities: Lines 8B, 9A, 8A, 9C, and 10A. Lines 8B, 8A, and 8C rise because they relate to internal organ defects with high weights (AHP score 11.71%). Line 6A falls from 2nd to 10th and Line 5A from 4th to 12th, because they relate only to feather defects with low weights (6.36% and 5.85%). Mitigation actions are therefore tiered. High priority under both FRPN and the integrated score: Line 9A (preventive maintenance schedule and daily checklist), 9C (scheduled spare parts replacement), and 8B (periodic training with evaluation). High priority under FRPN only: Line 6A (plucker machine repair and rubber finger inspection) and 5A (scalding temperature monitoring with a calibrated thermometer). High priority under the integrated score only: Line 8A (mentoring of new operators by senior operators) and 10A (evaluation and rearrangement of the workflow). Next come job rotation and scheduling (Line 8C), a calibration schedule with records (Line 9B), size sorting before hanging (Line 7A), and a crate unloading procedure (Line 3A). Lines 1A, 2A, 4A, and 10B only need routine monitoring. Current controls detect or react to failures, so PT XYZ needs to move toward prevention.

This study has four limitations. It covers one company and five internal respondents, and the S, O, and D values are subjective. The mapping of lines to defect types is an initial assumption and α = 0.60 was chosen without a sensitivity test. We set the membership functions and rule base without sensitivity analysis or validation against post-improvement data, and did not analyze the cost or implementation of the recommendations. The superiority of FRPN over RPN is therefore unproven, and the findings apply only to PT XYZ and the data analyzed.

Further research could combine fuzzy AHP with longer time series of production and defect data, test the Sugeno fuzzy method, and add system simulation to search for the best improvements. Researchers also need to examine how operator precision and working conditions affect the evisceration process, and validate the line-to-defect mapping with process experts. Cost analysis, sensitivity analysis of the membership functions and the α value, and post-implementation evaluation of the recommendations will show how effective the results are.
